<div class="paper-what">
  <div class="paper-section-label">What this paper does</div>

Once an LLM finishes training, its knowledge is frozen. It has no access to later information, and tends to confidently fabricate details not in its training data. That's hallucination.

The fix is simple: instead of asking the model to answer directly, first retrieve relevant documents from an external source, then have the model read them and generate an answer. Open-book exam, not closed-book.

The key advantage: you only need to update the knowledge database, not the model, to reflect new information. Tested on Wikipedia, it hit state-of-the-art on multiple open-domain QA tasks at the time.

</div>

<div class="paper-takeaway">
  <div class="paper-section-label">Takeaway</div>
  <div class="takeaway-item">
    <span class="ti-marker">◆</span>
    <span>The core design principle became the foundation for all subsequent RAG research. The model doesn't need to memorize everything; it needs to learn how to search well and how to read well.</span>
  </div>
  <div class="takeaway-item">
    <span class="ti-marker">◇</span>
    <span>For battery R&D AI systems specifically, RAG feels more important than in most domains. Battery research moves fast, and a model's parametric knowledge goes stale quickly. Being able to update the knowledge base with the latest literature, without retraining, isn't just a nice-to-have; it's probably the core architectural requirement.</span>
  </div>
